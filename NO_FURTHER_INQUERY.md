# Ohio Trusts: The "No Further Inquiry" Rule

These are my research notes, not legal advice. Confirm everything here with a trust-litigation attorney.

## Bottom line

- Ohio has the rule. It is written into the Ohio Trust Code at **R.C. 5808.02**, which is based on Uniform Trust Code §802.
- If a trustee deals with trust property **for his own account**, the deal is **voidable by an affected beneficiary**. The beneficiary does **not** have to prove the deal was unfair or made in bad faith. This is the true "no further inquiry" rule.
- Deals with the trustee's **spouse, children, siblings, parents**, the trustee's own attorney or agent, or his businesses are **presumed** to be conflicted. This is a softer rule: the trustee can try to rebut the presumption by showing the deal was fair. See [The trustee's brother (also a beneficiary)](#the-trustees-brother-also-a-beneficiary).
- The main escape routes are these: the trust document authorized the deal, a court approved it, the beneficiary consented or released the claim, or the **time limit ran out** (R.C. 5810.05, 2 or 4 years).

## What the rule is

A trustee owes the beneficiaries a duty of loyalty. The strictest form of that duty is the "no further inquiry" rule. When a trustee is on both sides of a deal involving trust property, the court does not ask whether the price was fair or whether the trustee meant well. The conflict itself is enough to undo the deal. The rule exists because self-dealing is hard for beneficiaries to detect and prove, so the law removes the temptation completely.

## Ohio statute: R.C. 5808.02 (duty of loyalty)

- **(A)** "A trustee shall administer the trust solely in the interests of the beneficiaries."
- **(B)** "a sale, encumbrance, or other transaction involving the investment or management of trust property entered into by the trustee for the trustee's own personal account or that is otherwise affected by a conflict between the trustee's fiduciary and personal interests is voidable by a beneficiary affected by the transaction unless one of the following applies:" (the exceptions are listed below).
- **(C)** A deal is **presumed** to be affected by a conflict if the trustee makes it with:
  1. the trustee's spouse;
  2. the trustee's descendant, sibling, or parent, or the spouse of one of them;
  3. an agent or attorney of the trustee;
  4. a corporation or other enterprise in which the trustee has an interest "that might affect the trustee's best judgment."
- **(D)** A deal the trustee makes personally, not involving trust property, is still a conflict if it takes "an opportunity properly belonging to the trust."
- **(F)** When voting stock or controlling a business the trust owns, the trustee must act in the beneficiaries' best interests.
- **(H)** A court may appoint a **special fiduciary** to decide on a proposed deal that might violate this section.

## The trustee's brother (also a beneficiary)

**Question:** Does the rule apply to deals between the trustee (Charles Lutz) and his brother, when the brother is also a beneficiary of the same trust?

**Short answer:** Yes, but in its softer, **rebuttable** form. The brother being a beneficiary does **not** remove the protection.

- **Siblings are named in the statute.** Under R.C. 5808.02(C)(2), a sale, encumbrance, or other transaction involving trust property between the trustee and his **sibling** is **presumed** to be affected by a conflict of interest. Nothing in the statute makes an exception because the sibling is also a beneficiary.
- **It is a presumption, not an automatic loss.** The official Uniform Trust Code comment to §802 (Ohio's 5808.02 is based on it) explains the difference:
  - Deals for the trustee's **own account** are "irrebuttably presumed" conflicted, and "It is immaterial whether the trustee acts in good faith or pays a fair consideration." This is strict "no further inquiry."
  - Deals with relatives such as siblings are "presumptively voidable, not void." "The presumption is rebutted if the trustee establishes that the transaction was not affected by a conflict between personal and fiduciary interests." Factors include "whether the consideration was fair" and whether the terms match "those that would be transacted with an independent party."
  - In practice: once you show a trust-property deal went to the brother, the **burden shifts to the trustee** to prove it was fair and at arm's length.
  - *The UTC comment is persuasive in Ohio courts but not binding. Confirm with an attorney how Ohio courts apply it.*
- **Could it be strict "no further inquiry" after all?** Possibly. If the deal with the brother really benefited the trustee himself (for example, a side arrangement, or the trustee sharing in the property afterward), it may count as a deal "for the trustee's own account." Then the strict rule applies. That depends on the facts.
- **The brother's consent protects only the brother.** The consent/ratification exception in 5808.02(B)(4) is personal to "the beneficiary." If the brother agrees to a deal with himself, that does **not** stop **other** beneficiaries from voiding it.
- **Normal distributions to the brother are different.** Paying the brother his share **under the trust's terms** is not self-dealing. But the trustee must still:
  - "act impartially in investing, managing, and distributing the trust property, giving due regard to the beneficiaries' respective interests" (**R.C. 5808.03**, the duty of impartiality); and
  - exercise any discretionary power "reasonably, in good faith, and in accordance with the terms and purposes of the trust and the interests of the beneficiaries" (**R.C. 5808.14(A)**).

  Favoring the brother in distributions, timing, or use of trust property can be a breach even when it isn't a "transaction" under 5808.02.
- **Check the trust document.** The trust can authorize deals with family members (exception (B)(1)). Also, the UTC comment notes that a grant of discretion over distributions to a class that includes the trustee "implicitly authorizes" distributions to the trustee. Whether the trust gives Charles discretion over distributions to himself or his brother matters.

**Examples likely to trigger the presumption:** selling, leasing, or giving trust real estate to the brother; lending him trust money; letting him live in or use trust property rent-free or below market; buying something from him with trust funds.

## Exceptions and defenses

A self-dealing deal is **not** voidable under (B) if any of these apply:

1. The **trust terms** or another Ohio statute authorized it.
2. A **court approved** it.
3. The beneficiary did not sue within the **time limit in R.C. 5810.05** (see below).
4. The beneficiary **consented, ratified, or released** the trustee under R.C. 5810.09.
5. The deal involves a contract or claim the trustee had **before** becoming, or planning to become, trustee.

Also:

- **(E)** An investment another statute permits is not presumed conflicted, as long as it meets the prudent investor rule (R.C. Ch. 5809).
- **(G)** The following are allowed **unless the beneficiaries establish that it is unfair**:
  - an agreement between trustee and beneficiary on the trustee's appointment or pay;
  - reasonable trustee compensation;
  - deals between the trust and another trust, estate, or guardianship the trustee also handles;
  - deposits in the trustee's affiliated bank;
  - money the trustee advances to protect the trust.

**Key point:** the trust document itself can authorize self-dealing (exception 1). Reading the full trust instrument is essential.

## Deadlines: R.C. 5810.05

- **2 years:** if the beneficiary was **sent a report** that adequately discloses the potential claim **and** tells them the time allowed to sue, the 2 years run from the date the report was sent.
- **4 years:** otherwise, 4 years from the **earliest** of:
  - the trustee's removal, resignation, or death;
  - the end of the beneficiary's interest in the trust;
  - the termination of the trust;
  - when the beneficiary knew, or reasonably should have known, of the breach.
- Equitable defenses such as **laches, estoppel, and waiver** can still bar a claim. Don't sit on it.

## Ohio case law

- ***Manchester v. Cleveland Trust Co.***, 168 N.E.2d 745, 84 Ohio Law Abs. 321 (Ohio Ct. App. 1960). A law review article on self-dealing trustees cites this case as applying the "no further inquiry" rule to set aside a self-dealing transaction once the breach is shown. *I verified the citation, but I could not read the opinion text. Ask an attorney to confirm how the case applies.*
- The case law predates the Ohio Trust Code (effective 1-1-2007). Today the statute, R.C. 5808.02, is the main authority.

## Related: the right to the trust document and reports (R.C. 5808.13)

This matters because **you can't spot self-dealing without the documents.**

- The trustee must keep current beneficiaries "reasonably informed" and must **promptly respond** to requests for information.
- **On request, the trustee must promptly furnish a copy of the trust instrument.** The trustee may send a redacted copy, but if you ask for the **entire** instrument, the trustee must provide it.
- Within **60 days** after accepting the trusteeship, or after learning of an irrevocable trust, the trustee must notify current beneficiaries. The notice must cover the trust's existence, the settlor, the right to a copy of the trust, and the right to reports.
- The trustee must send an **at least annual report** of trust property, liabilities, receipts, disbursements, and trustee compensation, with a list of assets and their market values if feasible.
- The request for the trust copy should go to the **trustee (Charles Lutz)**. The duty belongs to the trustee, not to the drafting attorney (Atty. Gernert).
- *The text above is from a 2016 copy of the statute. Check the current version at codes.ohio.gov.*

## Questions to ask the attorneys

- Has the trustee sold, leased, borrowed against, or personally used any trust property, such as real estate?
- Has any trust property gone to, or been dealt with by, the trustee's spouse, family, or businesses? (These deals are presumed conflicted under 5808.02(C).)
- Does the trust instrument authorize self-dealing or waive the duty of loyalty?
- Have I received anything that could start the **2-year clock** under 5810.05? If not, which 4-year trigger applies?
- Should we formally demand the full trust instrument and annual reports under 5808.13, and if so, how?
- Has any trust property been sold, leased, lent, or given to the trustee's brother, or used by him? If so, can the trustee prove it was at fair, arm's-length terms?
- Have distributions or use of trust property favored the brother over other beneficiaries (R.C. 5808.03, impartiality)?
- Is it worth asking the court for a special fiduciary under 5808.02(H), or for removal of the trustee?
