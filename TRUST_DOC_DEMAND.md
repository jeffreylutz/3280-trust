# Getting the Trust Paperwork: Beneficiary Rights in Ohio

These are my research notes, not legal advice. Confirm the right steps with a trust-litigation attorney.

**Situation:** I am a beneficiary of an irrevocable trust (trustee: Charles Lutz) and have not been given the trust paperwork.

**Bottom line:** Ohio law gives a beneficiary the right to the trust paperwork, and the duty to provide it belongs to the **trustee**. The usual order is: ask in writing, then have an attorney send a formal demand, then go to court if needed. In the meantime, there are public records to check.

## 1. Send the trustee a written request (start here)

Under **R.C. 5808.13**, the trustee must:

- **Promptly give a beneficiary a copy of the trust document on request.** The trustee may send only the parts he thinks are relevant to you. But if you ask for the **entire trust instrument**, he must provide all of it. Ask for the entire instrument, including all amendments and restatements.
- **Keep current beneficiaries reasonably informed** and promptly answer requests for information.
- **Send an at-least-annual report** of trust property, liabilities, receipts, disbursements, and his own compensation, with a list of assets and their market values if feasible.
- **Notify current beneficiaries within 60 days** after the trust became irrevocable, or after he accepted the trusteeship. The notice must cover the trust's existence, who created it, and the right to request a copy and to receive reports. This applies to trusts created or made irrevocable on or after January 1, 2007.

**How to send the request:**

- Send it by **certified mail, return receipt requested**, and by email if available. Keep copies of everything.
- Cite **R.C. 5808.13(B)(1) and (C)**.
- Request:
  - the entire trust instrument, with all amendments and restatements;
  - a current list of trust assets;
  - every annual report for the period he has served.
- Give a reasonable deadline, such as **30 days**.

The letter also becomes evidence if the trustee ignores it.

## 2. Have an attorney send a formal demand

A letter on law-firm letterhead often gets the documents without going to court. Any of the firms in `README.md` could do this. As of 2026-09-25, Fried Law Group is the furthest along.

## 3. Go to probate court if the trustee refuses

- **Which court:** Ohio probate courts can hear cases involving a living trust, meaning one created during the settlor's lifetime, like this one (**R.C. 2101.24(B)(1)(b)**).
- **Why the trustee is exposed:** ignoring a beneficiary's request is a breach of his duties as trustee. Under **R.C. 5810.01(B)**, the court can:
  - **order the trustee to perform his duties**, including producing the documents;
  - **order the trustee to account** for the trust;
  - suspend him, or appoint a special fiduciary to take over the trust property;
  - reduce or deny his compensation;
  - **remove him as trustee** (**R.C. 5807.06**). One ground for removal is "persistent failure of the trustee to administer the trust effectively."
- **Attorney's fees:** under **R.C. 5810.04**, the court may award costs, expenses, and reasonable attorney's fees to any party, "as justice and equity may require." They can be paid by another party or from the trust. The judge decides this case by case.

## 4. Look for records you can get on your own

These won't give you the full trust, but they can confirm what the trust owns and who controls it:

- **County recorder's office:** if the trust owns real estate, the **deed** is public. It may show the trust's name and date and the trustee's name.
- **Probate court records:** if the settlor has died and left a will that sends property into the trust, the will is usually filed with the probate court and is public.
- **Tax forms:** if you've received trust distributions, you may have **K-1 forms** that identify the trust and its tax ID.
- **Atty. Gernert (the drafting attorney):** his client is the settlor, not the beneficiaries, so he can decline to share the trust. That may be part of why he cited confidentiality on 2026-09-22. The legal right to the document runs against the **trustee**, not the drafting attorney.

## Time limits

Under **R.C. 5810.05**:

- **2 years:** if the trustee sends a report that adequately discloses a potential claim and tells you the deadline, you have 2 years from the date it was sent.
- **4 years:** otherwise, you generally have 4 years from the earliest of: the trustee's removal, resignation, or death; the end of your interest in the trust; termination of the trust; or when you knew, or should have known, of the breach.

If you've never received a report, the 4-year rule generally applies. Don't wait anyway: defenses such as laches, estoppel, and waiver can still apply if you delay.
